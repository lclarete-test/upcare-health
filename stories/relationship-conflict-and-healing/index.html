Can relationship conflict slow physical healing?

A laboratory study of married couples linked hostile conflict with slower healing of small experimental wounds. Here is what it can and cannot tell us.

By UpCare Health · Published October 10, 2026 · 3 min read

An argument can linger in the mind. In one unusual experiment, researchers asked whether it could also be measured in the body’s repair process.

How was the study designed?

Married couples completed two laboratory visits. Small controlled blister wounds were created on their forearms. During one visit they discussed a supportive topic; during another they discussed a disagreement. Researchers tracked wound healing and inflammatory signals.

What did the researchers find?

Wounds healed more slowly after the conflict discussion than after the supportive interaction. Couples who showed consistently higher hostility across discussions healed at about 60% of the rate observed in low-hostility couples.1

Does an argument damage your body?

The study does not mean that one disagreement causes illness or that healthy relationships never contain conflict. It involved a small group, controlled wounds and observed behaviors. It shows that social stress can coincide with measurable biological changes, not that every conflict produces the same effect.

What does that mean for you?

The useful question is not whether you argued. It is what happens around conflict: contempt, repeated hostility, safety, repair and support. Notice how your body feels before and after difficult conversations and whether the pattern is temporary or constant.

Research provides evidence that relationships and physiology are connected. Your experience provides the context. Persistent fear, coercion or hostility is not a communication trick to solve alone; support from a clinician, therapist or trusted service may be appropriate.
